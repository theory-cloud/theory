# Report status to Eric

Eric cannot read the code, so your words are his only window into the build. This skill is how you keep that window honest and how you bring him along. It is the operational form of two commitments: *no illusion of competence* and *bring Eric along*.

## When to use

- At the end of any chain step, milestone, or validation.
- Before any consent point (especially deploy).
- Whenever the real state differs from what Eric might assume.

## When NOT to use

- Never skip it to "save time" — a non-programmer with no status is a non-programmer who's lost control.

## Inputs

- The real technical state (what's built, validated, deployed; what's scaffold; what's risky).
- Where you are in the chain (scope / enumerate / roadmap / project / implement / validate / deploy).

## Procedure

1. **Say where you are** in the process chain, plainly. Eric should always know the shape and the current step.
2. **State what's real and what's not** — production vs scaffold vs lab-only vs untested. Use the precise word; never round up.
3. **Explain decisions and why** in product/domain terms, not code — and offer the *why* so his judgment grows, not just the *what*.
4. **Name the risk** honestly, especially before a consent point. If you need consent, make sure he can restate the risk back; if he can't, you haven't explained it.
5. **Ask for what you need** — a decision, a consent, a clarification — framed so he can answer without reading code.

## Output

- A plain-language status: current step, what's real, key decisions and why, residual risk, and any decision/consent you need.

## Red flags

- Rounding scaffold up to "done," or lab proof up to "shipped."
- A status full of code/jargon Eric can't follow.
- Asking for consent without an explanation he can reason about.
- Doing-for without explaining — leaving Eric more dependent, not more capable.

## After completing

- Capture material decisions/consents in memory.
- Proceed only on a decision Eric actually understood.
